Current rules for the 2026–27 school year

How Current Texas Attendance Works

A plain-language guide for parents to the 90% rule, excused and unexcused absences, and what to do when your child has a serious illness. It is based on the Texas Education Agency's 2026–2027 Student Attendance Accounting Handbook and the Texas Education Code.

Who Has to Attend

Unless the law gives a specific exemption, a child who is at least 6 years old (or younger and already enrolled in first grade) must attend school until their 19th birthday. Pre-K and kindergarten students must also attend once they are enrolled. If a child is under 6 on September 1, a parent may withdraw them without breaking the attendance law.

A student who keeps attending after turning 19 must also attend every day.

TEA handbook §3.5, p. 57; Texas Education Code §25.085

Earning credit

The 90% Rule

To receive credit or a final grade for a class, Texas students generally have to attend at least 90% of the days the class is offered. This is a separate question from whether an absence was excused. The TEA handbook says it plainly: "Numerous absences, whether excused or unexcused, may jeopardize a student's ability to receive credit or final grades for classes."

90% or more
In a 180-day year: 18 or fewer absences

The student meets the attendance requirement for credit.

75% to 89%
In a 180-day year: 19 to 45 absences

The student can still earn credit by completing a plan approved by the principal, such as makeup work or extra instructional time.

Below 75%
In a 180-day year: more than 45 absences

The student generally loses credit unless the campus attendance committee decides there were extenuating circumstances and sets a plan to earn it back.

Texas Education Code §25.092, cited in the TEA handbook §3.6.3 (p. 66) and §3.6.4 (p. 67). Absence counts are rounded and for illustration; credit is usually decided class by class.

What "excused" really means

Excused vs. Unexcused Absences

Parents often hear one word, "excused," used for three different things. Knowing the difference explains why a sick-day note doesn't always protect a student.

Excused

The School Accepts the Reason

A teacher, principal, or superintendent may excuse an absence "for any reason acceptable" to them. This keeps the absence from being treated as truancy. Ordinary illness with a parent note usually falls here.

Counted present

A Reason Listed in State Law

For a short list of reasons in the law, the student is counted as present for the day. These are the only absences the state treats as attendance, and the school must keep documentation.

Unexcused

No Accepted Reason

An absence the school does not excuse. Ten or more days or parts of days within six months in the same school year is truant conduct for students ages 12 to 18.

Why this matters for sick days

An ordinary illness like a cold, flu, or stomach bug is not on the state's list. The school can excuse it, but the student is still counted absent, and the day still counts toward the 90% rule.

Makeup Work

For most of the reasons above, the school must give the student a reasonable amount of time to make up missed work. If the student completes it, the day counts as a day of attendance for the compulsory attendance law.

Tardies

Tardies generally are not counted as absences when enforcing the compulsory attendance law.

Each district adopts its own tardy policy. Check your student handbook for details on how tardies are handled in your district.

TEA handbook §3.5, p. 58 and §3.6.3, p. 66

When your child is seriously ill

Serious or Life-Threatening Illness Certificates

If a serious or life-threatening illness, or its treatment, makes it impossible for your child to attend, those absences can be counted as attendance. You provide a certification from a physician licensed to practice in Texas, on a form your district adopts.

What the Physician's Certification Includes

  1. The student's illness
  2. A statement that the illness is serious or life-threatening
  3. How long the student is expected to be absent because of the illness or treatment
  4. A statement that the illness makes attending school infeasible during that time

Your district must adopt a form for this, and it cannot require more documentation or information than these four items.

Long-Term or Recurring Conditions

A student with a chronic illness or long-term health problem who will be confined to home or a hospital for at least four weeks may qualify for General Education Homebound services, where a teacher provides instruction at home. This includes conditions that come and go, as long as the time adds up to at least four weeks in the school year.

When the student is ready to return, a homebound or Section 504 committee sets a transition period based on current medical information.

Students with a 504 plan or an IEP who miss a lot of school should have their committee meet to plan for absences and prevent truancy.

TEA handbook §3.6.3, p. 65; §3.6.4, p. 67; §3.7.6–3.7.7, p. 73

Questions parents ask

Common Questions

My child had the flu and I sent a note. Does that day count against them?

For the 90% rule, yes. The school can excuse the absence, so it isn't treated as truancy, but ordinary illness isn't on the state's list of reasons a student is counted present. The day still counts as a missed day.

What if I take my child to the doctor in the middle of the day?

If your child goes to school before or after the appointment on the same day and you bring a note from the provider, they are counted present. Telehealth appointments count too.

My child has a chronic condition. What should I ask the school about?

Ask about General Education Homebound services if your child will be out at least four weeks in total, and about a Section 504 plan if your child doesn't have one. For a serious or life-threatening illness, ask for the district's physician certification form.

What happens if my child drops below 90%?

Between 75% and 89%, your child can usually earn credit by completing a plan the principal approves. Below 75%, the attendance committee decides whether there were extenuating circumstances. Talk to your campus attendance office early.

Who decides what counts as acceptable documentation?

The state sets the rules above, and each district writes a local policy for what documentation it accepts. Check your district's student handbook or ask your attendance clerk.

A proposal, not current law

A Proposal for 10 Protected Sick Days

Parents are asking Texas lawmakers to let up to 10 documented sick days a year not count against the 90% rule, with a parent note accepted and school funding protected.

The short version

What Would Change for Your Family

Under today's rules, students must attend 90% of class days to earn credit, and ordinary sick days count as missed days even when excused. Read how today's rules work.

How it works today

Sick Days Still Count

  • Students must attend at least 90% of the days a class is offered to receive credit.
  • Schools can excuse a cold, flu or stomach bug, but the student is still counted absent, even with a parent or doctor's note.
  • Only serious or life-threatening illnesses with a physician's certification are counted as attendance.
  • Schools generally don't receive state funding for days a student is out sick.
What the proposal would do

10 Protected Sick Days

  • Up to 10 documented sick days per student, per school year, would not count against the 90% rule.
  • A written note from a parent or guardian, turned in within 5 days of the absence, would count, so families don't need a doctor visit for a common bug. Medical notes would count too.
  • Public and charter schools would keep their funding for those days through a state adjustment, while still reporting real attendance.
  • Protected sick days would not trigger 90% warning letters. Makeup work and safeguards against misuse would stay in place.
  • Students who need more than 10 days for a serious, chronic or long illness would still get individual review.

Why it matters

Kids Shouldn't Have to Choose Between Getting Well and Getting Credit

Children should be able to recover from a real illness without families worrying about attendance penalties, and without schools losing funding. Sending a sick child to school spreads illness to classmates and teachers.

Regular school attendance is essential to student learning and is required by Texas law. This proposal does not change that expectation. It asks that, when a child is genuinely ill, attendance policy give priority to three things: the student's health and recovery, the safety of classmates and staff, and stable funding for the schools that serve them.

Contagious childhood illnesses are a normal part of growing up. A reasonable number of documented sick days, supported by a parent's note, should not put a student's course credit at risk or reduce a school's funding.

Questions parents ask

About the Proposal

Is this the law right now?

No. This is a proposal asking Texas lawmakers to change the law. Until they do, today's rules still apply at every school.

What about serious or long-term illnesses?

Today's law already counts absences for serious or life-threatening illnesses with a physician's certification as attendance. The proposal keeps that protection, and students who need more than 10 days would still get an individual review.

Would schools lose money?

Schools are funded partly on average daily attendance. The proposal asks the state to protect public and charter school funding for qualifying sick days, while schools keep reporting real attendance numbers.

Couldn't families misuse this?

The proposal caps protected days at 10 per year, requires a written note to attendance staff within 5 days of the absence, keeps makeup-work expectations, and keeps safeguards against misuse.
